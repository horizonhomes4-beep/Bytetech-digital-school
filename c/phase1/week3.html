Phase 1 · Week 3Practical intensive — five sessions

The Internet & Browsers

You can now operate and manage the machine. This week you connect it to the world. You will learn to use a browser like a professional, find reliable information fast, recognise unsafe websites before they cause harm, and send email with confidence. As always, this is 100% hands-on.

Three rules for this week

1. Do it, don't read it. Only tick a box after you have physically done the action on a computer.

2. Practise safely. Never type a real password, card number or personal details into a page you are not sure about. All “unsafe site” practice this week uses trusted sites, screenshots and examples.

3. Ask before you change a shared computer. On a lab, work or borrowed PC, get permission before changing browser settings or creating accounts, and sign out and clear any bookmarks you made when you finish.

What you need

  • A computer with a working internet connection every day this week
  • At least one web browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari)
  • An email address for Day 5 (your teacher can help you create one if you have none)
  • Access to a second computer for the “Prove it” boxes
  • A notebook or a text file for writing your notes and answers
  • About 45–60 minutes per day
DAY 1 OF 5

How the Internet & Browsers Work

Basic

Most people use the internet every day without knowing what they are looking at. Today you learn the difference between the internet, a website, a browser and a search engine, and you learn to read a web address the way a professional does. Everything else this week builds on this.

Basic tasks

A browser is the app that opens websites (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari). A search engine (Google, Bing, DuckDuckGo) is a website that helps you find other websites. Write one sentence in your own words explaining the difference, then say it aloud to someone or to yourself.

Open your browser and find: the address bar, the tab strip, the back, forward and refresh buttons, the home button (if shown) and the menu (three dots or lines). Point to each one with the mouse and say its name.

Click the address bar, type wikipedia.org and press Enter. Notice that you did not need to type https:// or www. Now use the Back and Forward buttons, then press Refresh (or F5).

Open any Wikipedia article and look at the address. Identify the protocol (https://), the domain (en.wikipedia.org) and the page path (the part after the first single slash). Write the three parts down. The domain is the part that tells you who really owns the site.

Click the small icon at the left of the address bar (a padlock or settings icon). Read what it says about the connection. Remember: a padlock only means your connection is private. It does not mean the website is honest. You will use this on Day 4.

Open the browser menu → Help or Settings → About. Read the version number and whether it says it is up to date. If an update is waiting, note it down, and only install it if you have permission on this computer.

Advanced tasks

If a second browser is already on the computer (for example Edge and Chrome), use it. Otherwise, only if you have permission, download one from its official website (remember Week 2, Day 3: official source only). Open the same website in both and compare how the tabs and menus look.

In Settings, find On startup (or Home page). Choose what opens when the browser starts. Set it to something useful, close the browser completely, reopen it and confirm it worked. Ask permission first on a shared computer and change it back if it is not your machine.

In Settings → Search engine, see which search engine your browser uses. Switch to a different one, search for the same word in both, and note one difference in the results. Then set it to the one you prefer.

Prove it — on a different computer

On a second computer, open its browser and find the address bar, the menu and the About page in under one minute. Which browser is it and what version? Write it down.

DAY 2 OF 5

Tabs, Bookmarks, History & Downloads

Basic

Professionals do not keep one page open and hunt for things again and again. Today you learn to work with many tabs, save pages so you never lose them, find where you have been, and download files safely and find them again. Keyboard shortcuts are shown for Windows; on a Mac use Cmd instead of Ctrl.

Basic tasks

Open three tabs using the + button (for example a news site, Wikipedia and a search page). Click between them, drag one tab to a new position, then close one using the small ×.

New tab: Ctrl + T. Next tab: Ctrl + Tab. Close tab: Ctrl + W. Practise each one three times until you do not need to look at the list.

Close a tab on purpose, then press Ctrl + Shift + T to reopen it. This one shortcut will save you many times.

Open a page you like and press Ctrl + D (or click the star). Give it a clear name and save it. Then open a new tab, find the bookmark in the bookmarks menu (or Ctrl + Shift + O) and open it.

Press Ctrl + H to open the history. Find a page you visited earlier today and reopen it from there. Notice that the history is a list of everything, so anyone using this browser profile can see it.

Go to a site that offers a small free document (for example a sample PDF from a government or university website). Click the download link, watch the download indicator, then open the Downloads list (Ctrl + J) and click Show in folder. Say aloud where the file is stored on the computer.

Advanced tasks

Open the Bookmark Manager. Create a folder called Practical PC Course and move your bookmark into it. Add two more useful pages to the same folder. Show the bookmarks bar (Ctrl + Shift + B) and drag the folder onto it.

Right-click a tab and choose Pin tab. Notice it becomes small and stays at the left. Open a page that plays a video or sound, right-click its tab and choose Mute site. Unpin and unmute when you are done.

Open a private window (Ctrl + Shift + N in Chrome/Edge; Ctrl + Shift + P in Firefox). Visit a page, close the window, and check that it is not in the history. Then write down what private mode does not do: it does not hide you from your school, employer, internet provider or the website itself.

In Settings → Downloads, see the download location. If you have permission, change it to a folder you made yourself (for example Documents\Course Downloads), download a file again and confirm it landed there.

Prove it — on a different computer

On a second computer, open three tabs, bookmark one page, reopen a closed tab and find where downloads go. If it is a shared computer, delete the bookmark you made when you finish.

DAY 3 OF 5

Searching Effectively

Basic

Anyone can type words into a search box. The skill is getting the right answer in the first minute and knowing whether to trust it. Today you learn to choose keywords, use a few simple search tricks, filter results, and check a source before you believe it.

Basic tasks

Take the question “What is the best way to keep my laptop battery healthy?” Search it as a full sentence, then search it again with only the important words (for example laptop battery health tips). Compare the results and note which was better.

Search for a product such as laptop bag. Find the results marked Ad or Sponsored at the top. Say aloud why an advert is not the same as the best answer.

Search for computer skills course with no quotes, then with quotes around it: "computer skills course". Note how the results become stricter.

Search for jaguar speed, then jaguar speed -car. Notice how the minus sign removes results you do not want.

Type site:bbc.com weather (or another trusted site you know). This limits results to that one website. Try it with a second website.

Search for a news topic. Use Tools or Filters to show only results from the past year, then click the Images tab and the News tab. Say when a filter by date matters.

Advanced tasks

Type computer basics guide filetype:pdf. Open one of the results and check that it really is a PDF. Notice the address of the site before you open it.

Search for a surprising claim you have heard (for example about a health tip, a famous person or a scientific fact). Open three different, unrelated sources. Write down what each says and whether they agree. Decide whether to believe the claim, and why.

Pick one site from your results and find its About or Contact page. Answer: who runs it, what do they want (information, sales, opinion), and when was the page last updated? Write down whether you would trust it and why.

Choose one factual question (for example the population of Nigeria). Find the answer once by using a search and once by browsing directly to a trusted website. Which was faster? Which was more reliable?

Prove it — on a different computer

On a second computer, search for the same question as one of today's tasks and use quotation marks, site: and the time filter. Compare its default search engine with yours.

DAY 4 OF 5

Spotting Unsafe Websites & Browsing Safely

Basic

Most online harm comes from a person clicking something they should not have, not from clever hacking. Today you learn what fake and dangerous websites look like, what to do about pop-ups and download buttons, and how to control what websites can see. All practice today uses screenshots, examples and trusted sites. You will never enter your details on a suspicious page.

Golden rule: Never enter a password, card number or one-time code on a page you reached by clicking a link in a message. Type the address yourself or use a bookmark you made.

Basic tasks

Look at these two addresses: https://accounts.google.com/login and https://google.com.secure-login.example/login. The real owner is the domain just before the first single slash, working backwards from the end of the domain. Write down which one is real and why the other one is fake.

Write down three ways a scammer can fake an address, for example paypa1.com (a number 1 instead of an L), amazon-support.net (extra words) and gooogle.com (an extra letter). Then look at your own bookmarks and check each domain is spelled correctly.

Make a checklist of five warning signs: a browser warning page, spelling mistakes, urgent threats (“your account will be closed!”), prizes you did not enter for, and requests for passwords, card details or a fee to receive money. Keep the checklist open in another tab.

Never click the button inside a scary pop-up. Practise this: if a pop-up will not close, close the whole tab with Ctrl + W, or press Alt + F4 to close the browser. Say aloud what you would do if a pop-up says “Your computer has a virus, call this number.”

Search for a free program from a trusted software website. Look at the page: often several big buttons say Download but only one is real. Point to any button that is actually an advert. Remember the rule from Week 2: use the official site of the program.

Click the padlock/settings icon at the left of the address bar and open Site settings. Find Location, Camera, Microphone and Notifications. Say aloud why a simple website should not need your camera.

Advanced tasks

Open Settings → Privacy and security → Site settings → Notifications. Find a site allowed to show notifications and set it to Block if you do not need it.

In Settings, open Delete browsing data (or Ctrl + Shift + Del). Read the options: history, cookies, cached files. Say what each one means. Only clear the cached images and files for the last hour so you do not lose saved logins on a shared computer.

Hover the mouse over any link (without clicking) and read the address at the bottom-left of the browser. Practise on three links on a news website. Explain why a link's text can say one thing while the address goes somewhere else.

Your teacher (or you, using search) will give you five example addresses or screenshots. Classify each one as safe, unsafe or unsure, and write one reason for each. When in doubt, the correct answer is: don't click, ask.

Prove it — on a different computer

On a second computer, check the permissions of one website and count how many websites are allowed to send notifications. Does the browser show you a warning if you open a known test-warning page? (Only if your teacher provides one.)

DAY 5 OF 5

Email Basics & Final Project

Basic + Project

Email is still the way the working world communicates: job applications, bank notices, school letters, account recovery. Today you learn the parts of an email account, how to write and send a clear message with an attachment, and how to keep your inbox tidy. Then you bring the whole week together in one project. (Professional email etiquette and filters come in Week 7.)

Basic tasks

Open your email (webmail such as Gmail, Outlook or Yahoo, or an email app). Find and say aloud: Inbox, Sent, Drafts, Spam/Junk, Trash and the Compose / New message button. If you do not have an email account, ask your teacher to help you create one; use a strong, unique password that you do not use anywhere else.

An address has two parts: the username, the @ sign and the domain (for example name@example.com). Write your own address and circle each part. Say why one wrong letter sends your message to a stranger.

Click Compose. In To put your own address. Write a Subject that says what the message is about (not just “Hi”), then a short body: greeting, one clear sentence, a thank-you and your name. Send it and wait for it to arrive in the Inbox.

Open the email you received. Click Reply and type one line, then send. Then click Forward and send it to your teacher or a practice partner. Say aloud the difference between Reply and Forward.

Compose a new email. Use the paperclip (Attach) button to attach the file you downloaded on Day 2. Check that the file name appears in the message before sending. Never send an attachment without mentioning it in the body.

Open the Spam or Junk folder. Read one message subject. Do not click links inside it. Say aloud why spam is dangerous. Then check that the Report spam or Block option exists.

Advanced tasks

To = the main person. Cc = others who should see it. Bcc = others who receive it, but nobody can see their address. Send a practice message with one address in To, another in Cc and (if allowed) a third in Bcc. Say when Bcc is the right choice.

In your email Settings, find Signature. Create a simple professional signature (name, role or course, contact). Compose a new message and check that the signature appears.

Open a phishing example from your teacher, or search for “phishing email example” and look at a picture. Point out: the sender's real address, urgent language, a mismatched link (hover, don't click) and a request for a password. Write down what you would do: delete it, report it, do not reply.

On any shared or public computer, always sign out when you are finished, and close the browser. Find the sign-out button, use it, and confirm the sign-in screen appears again.

Final project: “Research & Report”

Choose one everyday topic (for example: how to protect a smartphone, how to write a good CV, or a health, sports or local-history topic). Do all of the following:

Use quotation marks, site:, the minus sign or a time filter. Write down which techniques you used.

Create a bookmarks folder named after your topic and save three sources you checked with the Day 3 and Day 4 tests.

Download a small document or image that supports your topic. Confirm it is in your Downloads folder.

Write a clear email with a subject and 4–5 sentences summarising what you learned, list the three source websites, attach the file and send it to your teacher (or to yourself if your teacher asks).

Open your sent email on another computer, then search for the topic there. Sign out completely when done.

Prove it — on a different computer

On a second computer, sign in to your email in the browser (only if you trust the computer), send yourself one message with an attachment and then sign out completely. Note anything that looked different.

End of week self-assessment

Answer honestly. If you can't say yes to all eight, revisit that day before requesting Week 4.

  • I can explain the difference between a browser and a search engine
  • I can break a web address into protocol, domain and path
  • I can work with several tabs, bookmarks, history and downloads
  • I can find a downloaded file on my computer
  • I can search with quotes, minus, site: and filters, and check a source
  • I can spot a fake address, a scam pop-up and a phishing email
  • I can write, attach, send, reply and forward an email, then sign out
  • I completed the final project on a second computer
